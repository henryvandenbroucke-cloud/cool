# Voiceover Script

**Title:** Every "Bad Luck" Moment That's Actually Math

**Thumbnail headline:** **NOT BAD** (black) **LUCK** (red)
**Length:** about 1,560 words, roughly 9:30–10:30 depending on your pace. That's over 8 minutes, so mid-roll ads are allowed.

### How to record
- Read only the normal text. Lines in *(italics in brackets)* are what's on screen; don't read them.
- `/` marks a short pause and `//` a longer, dramatic pause.
- Record each chapter as its own file (`00-intro.wav`, `01-toast.wav` … `11-outro.wav`) so retakes are easy.
- Talk like you're telling a friend, not reading a textbook. Smile on the jokes.

## 00 · COLD OPEN (0:00)
*(First frame = the thumbnail grid. Then the camera dives into circle 1. Fast cuts, one per line.)*

You drop your toast. / Butter side down. / Of course.

You pick the shortest line at the supermarket / and it's the only one that stops moving.

Your bus is late. Again. The lane next to you is flying. And somehow, everyone on your phone is happier than you.

At this point you're starting to think the universe has a personal problem with you. //

It doesn't. Every single one of these has a real explanation, and most of them are math.

And here's the part that's going to mess with your head: half of the things on this list are secretly the same thing. One hidden rule. By the end of this video you'll start seeing it everywhere / and number ten might change how you look at your friends.

Let's start with breakfast.

## 01 · YOUR TOAST ALWAYS LANDS BUTTER SIDE DOWN
*(Mascot knocks toast off a table. Slow-mo half-flip. SPLAT.)*

Number one: your toast always lands butter side down.

This sounds like a joke, but a physicist named Robert Matthews actually studied it, published it in a real physics journal in 1995, and won an Ig Nobel Prize for it.

Here's what's going on. When toast slides off a table, it doesn't just fall. It starts to spin. And a normal table is only about waist high. That's just enough height for the toast to make about half a flip before it hits the floor.

Butter side up / half a flip / butter side down.

It's not the weight of the butter. It's the height of the table. Want to fix it? You'd need a table about three meters tall. / Or, you know / just catch it.

The universe isn't against your breakfast. Your table is just the wrong size.

## 02 · THE OTHER LINE IS ALWAYS FASTER
*(Three checkout lines. Our guy's cashier holds up a "PRICE CHECK" sign.)*

Number two: the other line always moves faster.

You picked your checkout line carefully. Shortest one. Nice cashier. And then / someone needs a price check.

Here's the brutal math. If there are three lines, only one of them can be the fastest. So your chance of being in the fastest line is one in three. Which means two times out of three, some other line is going to beat you.

And there's a second trick. When your line is moving, you're busy: unpacking, paying, bagging. You don't even notice you're winning. But when you're stuck, all you can do is stare at everyone else moving.

So you remember every loss / and forget every win.

## 03 · YOUR BUS IS ALWAYS LATE
*(A timeline strip with buses. Gaps of 2 and 18 minutes. Orange pins rain down, 9 out of 10 into the big gap. The counter goes 5.0 → 8.2.)*

Number three: your bus is always late. And this one is where it gets weird.

Say a bus comes every ten minutes, on average. If you show up at a random time, you'd expect to wait about five minutes, right? Half the gap.

But real buses bunch up. Imagine one gap is two minutes, and the next one is eighteen. Still ten on average.

Now picture yourself as a pin, dropped onto the timeline at a random moment. Where do you land? The eighteen-minute gap is nine times bigger than the two-minute one / so nine times out of ten, you land in the long gap.

Do the math, and your average wait isn't five minutes. // It's eight point two.

The bus company isn't lying about its average. You just don't experience the average. You experience the gap you fall into, and big gaps are easier to fall into.

Remember that pin. It's going to keep showing up.

## 04 · IT'S ALWAYS IN THE LAST PLACE YOU LOOK
*(Fast, comedic. Mascot finds keys, then keeps opening drawers anyway.)*

Number four, a quick one: you always find things in the last place you look.

Well / yeah. Because once you find it, you stop looking.

Nobody finds their keys and goes, "Let me just check two more drawers."

Okay. Moving on. / Things are about to get personal.

## 05 · EVERYWHERE YOU GO IS PACKED
*(Packed gym, packed restaurant, then airplanes: three nearly empty, one packed. The pin drops on a random passenger and lands in the packed plane.)*

Number five: everywhere you go is packed. The gym. The restaurant. Your flight.

Airlines actually complain that tons of their flights are half empty. Passengers complain that every flight is full. And here's the thing: they're both right.

Remember the pin? If you drop a pin on a random passenger, which plane is it more likely to land on? The packed one, because that's where most of the passengers are. An empty flight has nobody on it to complain.

Same with college classes. Using Purdue University's own numbers, the average class has about thirty-five students. But look at it from the students' side, and the average student sits in a class of about ninety-three. A giant lecture gets counted by every single person in it. A tiny seminar barely gets counted at all.

So the crowd isn't following you. / You're just usually part of it.

## 06 · THE NEXT LANE ALWAYS MOVES FASTER
*(Top-down highway: cars spread out when fast, bunch up when slow. Our car is stuck in a clump.)*

Number six: the lane next to you always moves faster.

In 1999, researchers showed student drivers a video of traffic. About seventy percent said the next lane was going faster, even though it was actually going a little slower.

Why? Cars bunch together when they slow down and spread out when they speed up. So you spend way more time stuck in the slow, crowded stretches, watching other cars pass you, than you spend cruising past them. And the cars you pass disappear behind you. The ones passing you stay right in your face.

Sound familiar? / It's the pin again. You spend most of your time where it's slow.

## 07 · CREEPY COINCIDENCES
*(Mascot thinks of an old friend and a thought bubble appears. DING: a text from that exact person. Eerie music sting.)*

Number seven: creepy coincidences.

You think about someone you haven't talked to in years / and they text you that same day. That has to mean something, right?

There's an idea called Littlewood's law. Say a one-in-a-million event counts as a miracle. Now count every little thing you notice in a day: every sound, every thought, every notification. Roughly one per second, for all the hours you're awake. That adds up to about a million things a month.

Which means you should expect a one-in-a-million moment roughly once a month. Just by being alive and paying attention.

Coincidences aren't rare. / You just only remember the ones that hit.

## 08 · ATTRACTIVE PEOPLE ARE ALWAYS JERKS
*(A 2×2 chart: "looks" vs "nice". A diagonal line cuts off the bottom-left corner as "never noticed". The remaining dots form a downward slope.)*

Number eight: why do the attractive ones always seem to be jerks?

This one is called Berkson's paradox. Imagine you'd only date someone who's really good-looking, or really nice, or a decent mix of both. If they're neither, you never even notice them.

Now look at who's left in your dating pool. The nice ones don't need to be stunning to make the cut. But the rude ones? The only way they got in was by being extremely good-looking.

So among the people you actually date, looks and kindness seem to go in opposite directions. But that pattern isn't out there in the world. / You created it, with your own filter.

Your type isn't cursed. Your filter is just doing math behind your back.

## 09 · YOUR FRIENDS HAVE MORE FRIENDS THAN YOU
*(Network of doodle people. One popular person lights up with lines to everyone, and their face keeps reappearing in everyone's friend list.)*

Number nine: your friends have more friends than you do. And no, it's not because you're boring.

In 1991, a sociologist named Scott Feld noticed something strange: for most people, their friends have more friends than they do, on average.

Think about the most popular person you know. They're on a lot of people's friend lists, so they get counted over and over again, in everyone's average. The quiet person with three friends? Counted three times.

It's the pin one more time. When you meet people through friendships, the super-connected ones keep showing up.

On Twitter, researchers found this was true for more than ninety-eight percent of users.

## 10 · EVERYONE ONLINE IS HAPPIER THAN YOU
*(Phone feed of smiling faces → zoom out: the feed is only the brightest, most-connected corner of the network. Then a flip: a campus map, two flu curves, the friends' curve rises two weeks earlier.)*

And number ten: everyone online seems happier than you.

In 2017, researchers analyzed tens of thousands of Twitter users. They found that more popular people tended to post happier messages. And since your friends are, on average, more popular than you, most people were following friends who seemed happier than they were.

So when you scroll and it feels like everyone's life is better than yours / you're not seeing everyone. You're seeing the people who show up the most. The loudest, most connected, happiest-looking slice of the world.

But here's the twist. // This exact same trick can save lives.

In 2009, Harvard researchers tracked a flu outbreak on campus. They picked random students, then asked each of them to name a friend. The friends, being more connected, caught the flu about two weeks earlier than the random group.

The same math that makes you feel left out / turned into an early-warning system.

## 11 · THE REVEAL / OUTRO
*(All 10 circles come back. 5 of them light up orange and connect with a line: bus, packed, lane, friends, feed. Title stamp: "THE INSPECTION PARADOX".)*

So, remember what I said at the start? Half of this list was secretly the same thing.

The bus. The packed plane. The next lane. Your friends. Your feed.

It has a name: the inspection paradox. Whenever you experience the world by bumping into it, the big things (the long waits, the packed rooms, the popular people) get counted way more than they should.

You don't see the average. / You see what you bump into.

So the next time it feels like life is unfair, ask yourself one question: is this really the whole world? // Or am I just the pin that landed in the big gap?

*(Bus pulls up behind the mascot.)*

Oh / and your bus is here.

If one of these got you, tell me in the comments which one. And if you want more things your brain gets wrong without you realizing, watch this one next.
